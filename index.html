About this project

Why I built this.

My name is Aagrah Singh, and I’m a student at Seven Lakes High School in Katy, Texas. For as long as I can remember, I’ve been drawn to medicine — not just the science of it, but the human side. The idea that a single doctor, a single appointment, can change the entire trajectory of someone’s life.

“I started noticing that the people who needed healthcare the most were often the ones least able to get it — and that felt deeply wrong to me.”

As I explored family medicine as a profession, I became fascinated by how primary care doctors serve as the true foundation of healthcare. They’re the first point of contact, the ones who catch problems early, manage chronic disease, and connect patients to everything else. But in too many communities — low-income, rural, and minority ones especially — that foundation simply doesn’t exist.

I created The Access Gap to put that problem in plain sight. Not with jargon or policy-speak, but with real data and honest storytelling. I’m not a doctor yet — but I believe that raising awareness is a first step toward real change, and that students like me have a role to play in that conversation.

The Crisis

Four reasons the system fails.

Healthcare inequality isn’t one problem — it’s a web of overlapping barriers. Understanding each one is the first step to dismantling them.

01 —

Geographic deserts

Hundreds of rural counties have zero primary care physicians. Patients may drive hours for a routine visit — or skip care entirely and end up in the ER when things get critical.

→ 7,200+ federally designated shortage areas
02 —

Cost as a barrier

For uninsured patients, a single primary care visit can cost hundreds out of pocket. Many Americans delay or skip care not because they don’t want it — but because they simply can’t afford it.

→ 1 in 4 uninsured adults skipped care due to cost
03 —

Racial & ethnic disparities

Black and Hispanic adults are significantly less likely to have a usual source of primary care. These gaps trace back to decades of structural inequity in how healthcare was built — and who it was built for.

→ 23% access gap: White vs. Hispanic adults
04 —

Physician shortage

Medical graduates increasingly choose higher-paying specialties over family medicine. The U.S. faces a shortage of up to 48,000 primary care doctors by 2034 — hitting underserved communities hardest.

→ Up to 48,000 shortage projected by 2034 (AAMC)
Why?

Family medicine is the foundation of a healthy society.

Countries with strong primary care consistently show lower costs, better outcomes, and more equitable health across populations. When family medicine is inaccessible, everything downstream becomes more expensive and less effective. Prevention collapses. Chronic disease spirals. Emergency rooms overflow.

What can be done

Pathways toward equity.

The gap is real — but it isn’t permanent. Evidence-backed solutions exist at every level. Here are six of the most impactful.

1.

Federally Qualified Health Centers

Over 1,400 FQHCs provide sliding-scale primary care regardless of insurance status. Expanding their funding is one of the fastest ways to close the access gap in underserved communities.

2.

NHSC loan forgiveness

The National Health Service Corps repays medical school debt for physicians who commit to practicing in shortage areas — making family medicine a far more financially viable career path.

3.

Medicaid expansion

States that expanded Medicaid under the ACA saw significant improvements in primary care access among low-income adults. Full national expansion would extend those gains to millions more.

4.

Telehealth as a bridge

Remote primary care visits dramatically improve access for rural patients and those with mobility limitations. With proper reimbursement structures, telehealth can genuinely complement in-person care.

5.

Team-based care models

Integrating nurse practitioners, physician assistants, and community health workers into family practices can extend reach without sacrificing quality — allowing one physician to serve a much larger patient population.

6.

Diversifying the physician workforce

Research shows physicians of color are more likely to practice in underserved communities. Targeted scholarships and pipeline programs can build a workforce that reflects and serves the full diversity of America.
